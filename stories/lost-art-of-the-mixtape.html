The Lost Art of the Mixtape

By C.B. Barkley · October 11, 2026 · 3 min read

Before playlists, before shuffle, before anyone could send a song with a tap, there was the mixtape: ninety minutes of cassette, two sides, and every song chosen on purpose. If you grew up GenX, you made one. Probably dozens.

Finger on the pause button

The classic method started with the radio. You'd sit by the stereo with a blank tape loaded and your finger hovering over Record and Pause, waiting for the DJ to finally play the song you'd been hoping for all afternoon. The trick was catching the first note without catching the DJ talking over the intro. You almost never got it perfect. Half the tapes of the 80s start a second too late or end with a station jingle, and somehow that's part of their charm.

If you had a dual-deck boombox, you could dub straight from another tape, which felt like moving up in the world. High-speed dubbing was faster, but everyone swore it sounded worse.

Side A, side B

A mixtape had structure. Side A opened strong, because that's what the other person would hear first. The last song on side A mattered, because there was a pause while they flipped it over. Side B could take more chances. And at the very end of side B, there was usually a little leftover tape, which you filled with something short, or left as a hiss that faded to silence.

Running out of tape in the middle of the last song was a rite of passage. So was rewinding to re-record over a song you'd changed your mind about, and accidentally clipping the start of the next one.

The J-card

The tape wasn't finished until the case was. The J-card, the folded paper insert, got the track list in your best handwriting, which got smaller and smaller as you ran out of room. A title went on the spine. Some people drew the cover. Some cut pictures out of magazines. Some just wrote a name in ballpoint pen: the person the tape was for.

A tape said something

That's the part that's hard to explain to anyone who grew up with streaming. A mixtape took hours to make. You couldn't skip around while you made it, so you had to sit through every song, in real time, while it recorded. Giving someone a tape meant you'd spent an evening thinking about them. A breakup tape, a road trip tape, a "you need to hear this band" tape, a tape for someone you liked but couldn't say it to: the songs did the talking.

And when you got one, you played it until it wore thin. You learned the order by heart. To this day, some of us hear the end of one song and expect the next one on the tape, not the next one on the album.

Where they went

Burned CDs came next, then playlists, and the mixtape quietly became a memory. A lot of them are still in shoeboxes in closets and attics, labels fading, the tape inside a little stretched. If you've still got one, pop it in. It won't sound perfect. It never did.
